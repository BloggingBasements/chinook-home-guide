Summer and Hail

Checking Attic Ventilation on Hot Days

By Rob Ferland  ·  September 24, 2026  ·  5 min read

On a hot, dry Calgary summer day, it is normal for the upper floor of a house to feel warmer than the main floor or the basement. Heat rises. But if the air conditioner seems to run almost nonstop just to keep the bedrooms comfortable, or if the ceiling feels warm to the touch, attic ventilation may not be doing its job. A well ventilated attic acts like a buffer, letting the worst of the heat that builds up under the shingles escape. If that hot air has no way out, it radiates down into the house, so the rooms feel warmer and the cooling bills climb.

The Basics of a Breathing Attic

Most homes in this area use a passive ventilation system. It works on convection, the natural movement of air, without fans or other mechanical parts. There are two main parts: intake vents and exhaust vents. The intake vents are usually in the soffits, the underside of the roof overhang. These let cooler outside air into the attic space. The exhaust vents sit at or near the peak of the roof. They might be ridge vents that run along the peak, square vents often called turtle or mushroom vents, or gable vents on the attic end walls. As the sun heats the roof, the air in the attic warms, gets lighter, and rises. It leaves through the higher exhaust vents, and that movement pulls in fresh, cooler air through the lower soffit vents. This steady, slow air flow keeps the attic from turning into an oven.

A Simple Inspection from the Ground

There is a lot you can see without climbing a ladder. On a clear day, walk slowly around the outside of the house and look up. Start with the soffits. Check the small vent holes or grilles. Are they open and clean, or filled with dust, cobwebs, or old paint? On some older homes, these vents have been painted so often that they are effectively sealed. Then look up at the roof. Find the exhaust vents near the peak. Watch for cracked plastic, broken pieces, or metal vents bent from hail in a way that could cut down airflow. This slow walkaround gives a good first picture. The goal is a clear path for air to get in low and leave high.

The View From Inside the Attic

The most direct check is from the attic hatch. Take care with this. Use a solid ladder and expect a wave of heat. Even with good ventilation, an attic will be hot on a summer afternoon, but it should feel like a hot room, not a blast from a furnace. If the air that hits you feels intensely hot and heavy, the ventilation likely is not working well.

I remember inspecting a house in Tuscany on a July afternoon. When I opened the attic hatch, the heat felt like standing in front of an open oven door. A quick look told the story. The insulation crew had blown in a deep layer of insulation, which is good, but it completely buried all the soffit vents around the perimeter. The attic had no way to breathe in, so the hot air just sat there and cooked all day.

From the hatch, without climbing all the way in, you can still see a few important things. Look toward the edges of the attic where the roof slopes down to the walls. You should see thin slivers of daylight at the soffit vents. If you see none, something is likely blocking them. Check for baffles, usually plastic or styrofoam channels stapled to the underside of the roof deck above the soffits. They hold the insulation back and keep the airway open. If you do not see baffles, there is a good chance insulation is pushed right up against the roof and covering those vents.

Common Issues and Possible Fixes

Many ventilation problems are within reach for a homeowner. If soffit vents are clogged on the outside, they can often be cleaned with a stiff brush and a shop vacuum. If they are painted shut, they need to be scraped open and then repainted with some care so the openings stay clear. If you see from the hatch that insulation is piled against the soffits, you can often pull it back yourself. It is a hot, dusty job, so a proper mask, gloves, and long sleeves matter. Missing baffles can be added later, but working them into place in a tight attic is awkward. For some people this is acceptable weekend work. For others, it is better to pay a crew that does it every day.

When You Need a Professional

Some situations call for a licensed trade. If roof vents are cracked from hail or worn from age, a roofing contractor should handle the repair or replacement. Roof work without proper safety gear is not worth the risk. If the attic needs a major insulation change or a top-up, an insulation company has the tools to do it quickly and correctly. They can also install baffles through the attic where they are missing. If the house simply does not have enough venting, a roofer can add soffit or roof vents to improve airflow. It helps to think of the house as a set of systems that affect each other. Just as it is useful to know how to keep your garage door working in deep cold, it pays to understand what your attic is doing in summer.

The attic is more than a dusty void above the ceiling. It is part of the roofing and cooling system. When it can breathe on the hottest days, rooms feel more comfortable, the air conditioner does not work as hard, and shingles are less likely to overheat. A few quiet checks now and then can either confirm that things are working or point out small problems before they turn into bigger ones.
