Foundations and Water

Spring is the Time to Check Your Foundation

By Rob Ferland  ·  September 15, 2026  ·  4 min read

After a long Calgary winter, the first signs of spring feel like a release. The snow pulls back, the sun has some warmth again, and you can hear water running. Most of that water comes from the melting snowpack in your yard, and it all wants to go one place: down. Spring is the season I always gave to foundations. When the ground thaws, it has a lot of force. The top layer of soil might be soft and muddy, but deeper down the frost can hang on for weeks, forming a frozen bowl that holds meltwater against your basement walls. This creates what engineers call hydrostatic pressure, which is just a technical way of saying a lot of water is pushing on your concrete.

In and around Calgary, there is a lot of clay in the soil. When clay gets wet, it expands. When it dries, it shrinks. This constant expansion and contraction puts its own kind of stress on a foundation. The spring melt starts that cycle for the year, and it is a good time to take a slow walk around your property and see how the house handled the winter freeze.

A Walk Around the Outside

Your first check should be of the parging, the thin coat of mortar applied over the visible concrete of your foundation at ground level. It's mostly cosmetic, but it tells a story. Look for areas where the parging has flaked off, bubbled, or formed new cracks. This can mean moisture is getting behind it from the soil. Small patch repairs are a reasonable homeowner project for a dry weekend.

Next, look at the ground itself. Does the soil slope away from your house on all sides? It should. A gentle, consistent slope is your best defense, sending water away before it can soak in near the foundation. If you see low spots where water is pooling near a wall, you have a grading issue. Sometimes this is a simple fix with a shovel and a few bags of topsoil to build the grade back up. Also check your window wells. They should be clear of leaves and debris so water can drain, not fill up like a bathtub against your basement window.

Clues From the Inside

Once you've inspected the exterior, head to the basement. You are looking for changes, things that were not there last fall. With a good flashlight, examine the concrete foundation walls. Hairline vertical cracks are often just from the concrete curing and are not a cause for alarm. What you need to watch for are horizontal cracks, wide cracks you can fit a coin into, or cracks that are wider at one end than the other. Also look for stair-step cracks in the mortar joints of a concrete block foundation. These point to movement.

Another sign of moisture is efflorescence. This is a fluffy or crystalline white deposit on the surface of the concrete. It happens when water seeps through the wall and evaporates, leaving mineral salts behind. It tells you water is getting through, even if you don't see liquid. While you're downstairs, check for doors that have started to stick or swing open or closed on their own. Then go upstairs and look for new cracks in the drywall, especially coming off the corners of door and window frames. These can be signs that the structure is shifting.

I remember a house in Silver Springs where the owner had built a raised garden bed right against the foundation. It looked good, but every spring it acted like a sponge, holding water against the concrete wall. The basement had a damp, musty smell they could never quite place until that bed was moved away from the house by a few feet.

What You Can Do and When to Call for Help

Many useful foundation protections are simple maintenance. Extending your downspouts so they discharge water well away from the house is an easy afternoon job. Cleaning your eavestroughs is another. So is fixing minor grading issues. Diligent maintenance is about preventing big problems by doing small things regularly, much like how your garage door works through the deep freeze when you keep up with simple checks.

If you find significant new cracks, like a long horizontal one in the basement wall, it's time for a professional opinion. My advice is to start with a licensed structural engineer. An engineer's job is to give you an impartial look at the problem and recommend a solution if one is needed. They don't sell the repair itself, so you get an unbiased view. A repair company might be needed later, but the engineer tells you what needs to be done. For homes that are still relatively new, some foundation issues may be covered by warranty. The provincial government publishes information on its Alberta new home warranty page that outlines what is covered and for how long. Checking your foundation each spring gives you a steady sense of your home's health from the ground up.
