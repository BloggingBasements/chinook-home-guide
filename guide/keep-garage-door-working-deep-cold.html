Keeping Your Garage Door Working Through a Calgary Freeze

By Rob Ferland  ·  September 4, 2026  ·  5 min read

There is a particular sound a garage door opener makes when it's struggling against a frozen door. It's a low groan, a complaint from the motor that tells you it's giving everything it has. Sometimes you get lucky and the door breaks free with a loud crack. Other times, the motor just gives up, leaving you with a car trapped inside or outside on a bitterly cold Calgary morning. In my years as an inspector, I saw this often. The problem is rarely the opener itself. The deep cold affects the entire system, from the metal parts contracting to the rubber seal freezing solid to the concrete pad.

The First Step: A Balanced Door

Before you touch a lubricant or adjust the opener, you need to know if your door is properly balanced. An opener is designed to do the work of a person, not a forklift. Its job is to replace you pulling the door up or down. The heavy lifting is done by the large springs. When those springs are correctly tensioned, the door feels almost weightless. In the cold, any imbalance is magnified. The opener has to work much harder to move a heavy, stiff door, and that's when it fails.

To check the balance, you first disconnect the opener. Pull the red cord that hangs from the track. This disengages the motor. Now, try to lift the door by hand. It should move smoothly and be manageable. Lift it about halfway and let go. A well balanced door will stay put, or move only a few inches up or down. If it slams shut or flies open, the spring tension is wrong. Adjusting garage door springs is not a do-it-yourself task. They are under extreme tension and can cause serious injury if they break or are handled improperly. If your door isn't balanced, that's your first and only step: call a qualified technician.

Lubrication for the Cold

With a balanced door, your next task is proper lubrication. Cold weather makes most lubricants thick and gummy, adding resistance that your opener has to fight. You want a lubricant that is designed for low temperatures. A silicone or white lithium grease spray works well. You do not want to use a heavy grease or anything that will get sticky and attract dirt.

The parts to lubricate are all the moving metal ones. Spray the hinges between the door panels, the pins that the rollers sit on, and the bearings at each end of the main torsion tube. You can also apply a light coat to the torsion springs themselves. This helps them operate quietly and prevents corrosion. One thing you should never lubricate is the tracks that the rollers run in. Spraying anything in the tracks just attracts dust and grit, creating a sludge that makes the problem worse. The rollers are meant to roll, not slide in grease.

Weatherstripping and the Frozen Seal

The most common cause of a stuck door in our climate is ice. After a chinook, melted snow and water will pool on the driveway right where the door comes down. When the temperature plummets again overnight, that water freezes the bottom rubber weatherstrip firmly to the concrete. The opener is usually not strong enough to break this seal.

I remember a home in Tuscany where the homeowner had tried to chip the door free with a shovel. He'd torn the rubber weatherstrip to shreds and put a dent in the bottom panel of the door. All that for a thin layer of ice that could have been prevented by sweeping the area clean after the last snowfall.

Inspect the rubber seal along the bottom of your door. If it is cracked, torn, or has become hard and brittle, it should be replaced. A pliable seal will conform better to the floor and is less likely to freeze hard. The simplest prevention is to keep that area of your driveway clear. After any snowfall or melt, a quick sweep or shoveling of the spot where the door lands can prevent a lot of trouble.

Small Adjustments to the Opener

Most modern garage door openers have settings for the closing force and the opening force. These are usually small dials or screws on the motor unit itself. In winter, the door system is naturally a bit stiffer and may require slightly more force to get moving. A small, incremental adjustment to the force setting might be all that is needed to get things moving reliably.

Check the owner's manual for your specific model to understand how to make these adjustments. Do not simply crank the force to the maximum. If the door requires a large increase in force, it indicates a bigger problem, like the balance issue I mentioned earlier. While you are looking at the opener, also take a moment to check the safety sensors. These are the two small boxes near the floor on either side of the door opening. Make sure they are pointed at each other and that their indicator lights are on and steady. They can get knocked out of alignment easily, which will prevent the door from closing.

Knowing When to Call a Professional

A homeowner can handle the cleaning, lubricating, and minor adjustments. These maintenance tasks will prevent many winter garage door problems. It is just as important to know when to stop and call for help. Any work involving the springs or cables is for a professional. If the door is damaged, has come off its tracks, or is making grinding noises you cannot solve with lubrication, it is time to make the call. It's the same logic as getting your furnace checked before the real cold arrives. An hour with a technician on a mild day is a much better situation than being stuck with a broken door on the coldest morning of the year.
