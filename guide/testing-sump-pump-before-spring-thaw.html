Spring Melt

Testing Your Sump Pump Before the Spring Thaw

By Rob Ferland  ·  September 9, 2026  ·  5 min read

The sun is starting to feel a little warmer in Calgary. Late winter days arrive where the light has changed, even if there is still a lot of snow on the ground. A chinook might roll through and melt a foot of snow in an afternoon, a reminder that the big spring thaw is not far off. All that water has to go somewhere, and for many homes, the first line of defence against a wet basement is a sump pump. It sits quietly in a corner, often forgotten, until the one day it is needed.

Understanding the Sump Pump System

In many Calgary neighbourhoods, builders install a perimeter drainage system called weeping tile around the home's foundation footings. This perforated pipe collects groundwater and carries it by gravity to the lowest point, which is the sump pit, also called a sump liner or crock, in your basement floor. Without this system, water pressure can build up against your foundation walls and eventually find a way inside.

Inside that pit sits the pump. Its job is to lift that water up and get it out of the house. The pump has a float switch. As water from the weeping tile fills the pit, the float rises with it. When the water reaches a certain level, the float triggers the switch, and the pump turns on. It draws the water out of the pit and sends it through a discharge pipe that leads outside. Once the water level drops, the float goes down and shuts the pump off. It is an automatic process that protects your home from the pressure of groundwater during a heavy rain or, more common at this time of year, a rapid spring melt.

A Visual Inspection of the Components

Before you test the pump's operation, a quick visual check can tell you a lot. Start by removing the cover from the sump pit. Look inside. Sometimes things fall in there, like children's toys, loose insulation, or dirt and sediment that has built up over time. The pit should be reasonably clear of debris so that it does not foul the pump's intake or jam the float mechanism. You can scoop out any solids with your hands or a small bucket.

Next, look at the pump itself. It should be standing upright and not tilted on its side. Check its power cord. The pump should be plugged into a ground-fault circuit interrupter, or GFCI, outlet. This is the type of outlet with the little "test" and "reset" buttons on it. It reduces the risk of electrical shock in a wet area. Make sure the cord is in good condition, with no signs of fraying or damage, and that it has a bit of slack, not pulled tight. Then follow the discharge pipe up from the pump. Ensure all connections are tight and that the pipe is securely fastened.

Performing a Simple Manual Test

With the visual check done, it is time to see if the pump will do its job. You will need a bucket or a similar container filled with water. The idea is to copy what happens during a heavy melt. Pour the water steadily into the sump pit, not so fast that you splash everywhere but fast enough to raise the water level. As you pour, watch the float. You should see it begin to rise. When it gets high enough, you should hear the pump turn on. It is usually a distinct humming sound.

I was in a home in Tuscany once where the owner was sure his pump was fine. It was only a few years old. We poured the water in, the float went up, and nothing happened. A faint buzzing, but no pumping. The pump had seized from sitting in damp conditions over the winter without running. A few taps on the housing with a rubber mallet was enough to free the impeller and it spun to life, but it reminded both of us that even newer equipment needs to be checked.

Once the pump starts, it should make quick work of the water in the pit, discharging it out the pipe. The sound should be a smooth whirring or humming. If you hear grinding or other loud mechanical noises, that is a sign of a problem. The pump should run until the water level drops significantly, at which point the float will lower and shut the pump off.

Checking the Exterior Discharge Line

Getting the water out of the basement is only half the work. You also have to make sure it is being sent to a safe place outside. Find where the discharge pipe exits your house. It is usually a plastic pipe that comes out through the rim joist just above the foundation wall. The end of this pipe must be clear. It can get blocked by deep snow, ice that has formed from a slow drip, or leaves and debris from the fall. A blocked discharge pipe means the pump will run, but the water has nowhere to go.

The water needs to be directed well away from your foundation. Most people attach a flexible hose or a rigid extension to carry the water away from the house onto a splash pad that helps with erosion. The ground around your foundation should slope away from the house. If your pump discharges water right next to the wall, much of it will just soak back into the ground and end up right back in the weeping tile. Be mindful of where the water goes. The City of Calgary has rules about drainage, so you should not send water onto a neighbour's property or across a public sidewalk where it can freeze.

When a Plumber's Help is Needed

This test is a useful diagnostic tool. If the pump does not turn on at all, or if it just hums or buzzes, it likely needs to be repaired or replaced. If it makes loud, troubling noises, the bearings or impeller may be failing. If the pump runs constantly and never shuts off, the float switch is probably stuck or broken. These issues are a good time to call a qualified plumber.

This yearly check is a small investment of time that helps protect your basement from water damage. It is a simple piece of seasonal maintenance, much like making sure your garage door still works in a cold snap. A little attention now can prevent a much bigger problem when the thaw comes.
